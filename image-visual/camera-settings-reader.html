The Camera Settings Reader is a free online EXIF data viewer that reads the metadata your camera or phone embeds inside every JPEG photo. Drop in a photo and it pulls out the exact aperture, ISO, shutter speed and focal length used for that shot, then breaks down what each camera setting actually means and how it affected the final image — no photography background required.

How to read EXIF data from your photo

1. Upload your photo

Drag and drop a JPEG file onto the upload area above, or click browse to select a photo from your device. Works with images straight from a DSLR, mirrorless camera, or phone camera roll.

2. Let it scan the metadata

The tool reads the embedded EXIF metadata instantly, right in your browser. There's no upload wait and no server round-trip — camera settings appear within a second or two of choosing the file.

3. Review the core settings

Aperture, shutter speed, ISO and focal length are surfaced first as the four core camera settings, each with a quick badge showing whether the value falls in a typical or notable range for that shot.

4. Tap a setting to learn more

Tap any of the four tiles to open a plain-English explanation of that setting — what it controls, how it affected this specific photo, and a practical tip for using it in your own shots.

5. Check the shot analysis

Below the core settings, a shot analysis section summarizes the shooting mode, lighting conditions, motion blur risk, flash usage, white balance, camera and lens model, and GPS location if present.

6. View or copy the full EXIF table

Expand "All EXIF Data" to see every metadata field the camera recorded, or use the Copy All button to grab the complete list as plain text for a report, forum post, or photography portfolio note.

Who uses an EXIF data reader

Photographers learning manual mode

Beginners moving off auto mode use it to reverse-engineer their own shots and understand exactly which aperture, shutter speed and ISO combination produced a well-exposed, sharp result.

Photo buyers and editors

Stock photo buyers, magazine editors and photo agencies check camera settings and lens data to verify image quality, authenticity and technical suitability before licensing or publishing a photo.

Students and photography teachers

Photography courses use real EXIF data as teaching material — comparing shutter speed and ISO across sample images is one of the fastest ways to understand the exposure triangle.

Anyone curious about a photo's origin

Journalists, researchers and everyday users check a photo's camera model, timestamp and GPS metadata to understand where and how an image was captured before sharing or citing it.

Example: reading a typical portrait shot

f/1.8 aperture

A wide aperture like f/1.8 lets in plenty of light and produces a shallow depth of field, blurring the background so the subject stands out — a common setting for portraits taken with a prime lens.

1/200s shutter speed

A shutter speed of 1/200s is fast enough to freeze a person's natural movement and eliminate camera shake when shooting handheld, without needing extra light.

ISO 200

A low ISO like 200 means the shot was taken in good light — daylight or a well-lit room — and the resulting image will have minimal grain or noise.

85mm focal length

An 85mm focal length is a classic portrait length. It gently compresses facial features and separates the subject from the background for a flattering look.

Understanding your camera settings

What is EXIF data?

EXIF (Exchangeable Image File Format) is metadata your camera writes into every JPEG. It records how the shot was taken — aperture, shutter speed, ISO, GPS, lens, camera model and more.

The Exposure Triangle

Aperture, Shutter Speed and ISO work together. Change one and you affect the others. Mastering the balance between them is the foundation of photography.

Why no data shows up?

Screenshots, social media downloads, and apps like WhatsApp strip EXIF. Always use the original file from your camera roll or SD card for the full metadata.

Focal length & crop factor

The focal length shown is the physical lens length. On crop-sensor cameras (APS-C), multiply by ~1.5× to get the full-frame equivalent field of view.
