Gross vs Net Salary: What's the Difference?

By Rebrixe Team · July 7, 2026 · 7 min read

A job offer says $65,000 a year, but the first paycheck lands and the amount is noticeably smaller than a twenty-sixth of that number. Nothing is wrong — the offer letter was quoting gross salary, and the paycheck is showing net salary, and the gap between the two is made up of taxes, insurance, and retirement contributions that never touch a bank account in the first place.

The confusion between gross and net pay shows up constantly: comparing job offers, budgeting off the wrong number, filling out a loan application, or converting an annual salary into an hourly rate. This guide breaks down exactly what separates the two figures, how to calculate one from the other, and the mistakes that trip people up along the way.

Quick Answer

Gross salary is the full amount an employer agrees to pay before anything is withheld, while net salary — also called take-home pay — is what's left after income tax, payroll taxes, and benefit deductions are subtracted. To go from gross to net, subtract federal and state tax withholding, Social Security and Medicare, and any retirement or insurance contributions from the gross figure.

What is gross salary vs net salary?

Every paycheck carries two numbers that describe the same job at two different stages of the payroll process.

  • Gross salary is the total compensation figure quoted in an offer letter or employment contract, before any tax or deduction is applied.
  • Net salary (take-home pay) is what actually deposits into a bank account each pay period, after income tax, payroll taxes, and any benefit deductions are subtracted.
  • Deductions are everything that separates the two, split between mandatory withholding (federal and state income tax, Social Security, Medicare) and voluntary withholding (health insurance premiums, retirement contributions, HSA/FSA elections).

Both numbers describe the same job at the same salary level — the difference is entirely in how much of that figure has already been routed toward taxes and benefits before it reaches the employee.

Why the distinction matters

Mixing up gross and net doesn't produce an error message — it just produces a number that answers a different question than the one being asked. That shows up in a few common places:

  • Comparing job offers. Two offers with the same gross salary can have very different take-home pay depending on state tax rates, benefit costs, and retirement contribution requirements.
  • Personal budgeting. Building a budget off gross salary instead of net pay overstates the money actually available each month, which can lead to overspending or missed savings goals.
  • Loan and mortgage applications. Lenders typically ask for gross income to calculate debt-to-income ratios, since it reflects earning capacity independent of personal deduction choices.
  • Hourly-to-salary conversions. Converting a salary into an hourly wage, or vice versa, is almost always done using gross figures, since deductions vary too much from person to person to build into a standard formula.
📊 Quick stat Net pay typically runs roughly a fifth to a third lower than gross pay once federal tax, state tax, and payroll taxes are combined — the exact gap depends heavily on income level, location, and benefit elections.

Step-by-step: calculating net salary from gross

Method 1: Calculate annual net salary from gross salary

  1. Start with the gross annual salary. This is the figure listed in the offer letter or employment contract, before any deductions.
  2. Subtract federal and state income tax withholding. These are calculated based on the employee's filing status, allowances, and applicable tax brackets.
  3. Subtract payroll taxes. Social Security and Medicare are withheld at fixed percentages of gross pay, regardless of filing status.
    Net Salary = Gross Salary - (Federal Tax + State Tax + Social Security + Medicare + Other Deductions)
  4. Subtract voluntary deductions. Health insurance premiums, retirement contributions, and HSA/FSA elections come out last, and the remainder is net salary.

Method 2: Calculate net pay per paycheck

  1. Divide gross annual salary by the number of pay periods. Common schedules are 12 (monthly), 24 (semi-monthly), 26 (biweekly), or 52 (weekly).
    Gross Pay Per Period = Gross Annual Salary / Number of Pay Periods
  2. Apply the same deductions on a per-period basis. Tax withholding and benefit deductions are typically spread evenly across each paycheck.
  3. Subtract the deductions from gross pay per period. The result is the net amount that lands in the bank account for that pay period.

Method 3: Convert an annual salary into an hourly rate

  1. Determine total annual working hours. A standard full-time schedule is 40 hours a week across 52 weeks, or 2,080 hours a year.
  2. Divide gross annual salary by total annual hours.
    Hourly Rate = Gross Annual Salary / 2,080
  3. Adjust for actual schedule if it differs. Part-time hours, unpaid leave, or a non-standard work week should replace the 2,080 figure with the actual annual hours worked.

Common mistakes with gross vs net pay

1. Budgeting off the gross figure

Treating the salary listed in an offer letter as the amount available to spend each month overstates real take-home pay by the full amount of tax and benefit withholding.

2. Comparing two job offers by gross salary alone

Identical gross salaries can produce different net pay once state tax rates, benefit costs, and retirement contribution requirements are factored in, so the higher gross offer isn't always the higher take-home offer.

3. Using net pay for a loan or mortgage application

Lenders generally request gross income, so submitting net pay instead can understate borrowing capacity and affect the loan terms offered.

4. Assuming a fixed percentage always separates gross and net

The gap between gross and net pay changes with tax bracket, filing status, state of residence, and benefit elections, so there's no single universal percentage that applies to every paycheck.

💡 Pro tip When comparing job offers or salary-to-hourly figures, always confirm whether a quoted number is gross or net — recruiters and job postings almost always mean gross, but take-home pay estimates from a paycheck calculator are net.
