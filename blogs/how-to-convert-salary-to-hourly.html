How to Convert Salary to Hourly Rate

By Rebrixe Team · July 7, 2026 · 6 min read

A job offer lists $65,000 a year. A current role pays $28 an hour. Comparing the two should be simple math, until the quick version — divide by 2,000, or 2,080, or whatever number sounds familiar — quietly ignores the two weeks of unpaid leave in the new role, or assumes a 40-hour week when the actual schedule is 37.5.

Converting a salary to an hourly rate is genuinely simple once the right annual hours figure is used instead of a rounded guess. This guide walks through the standard formula, the adjusted version that accounts for real time off, and the mistakes that make the number drift from what someone actually earns per hour worked.

Quick Answer

To convert an annual salary to an hourly rate, divide the salary by the total number of hours worked in a year — typically 2,080 for a standard 40-hour week with no unpaid time off. For example, a $52,000 salary divided by 2,080 hours equals $25 an hour. Adjust the 2,080 figure if the actual work week or paid time off is different.

What is a salary-to-hourly conversion, and what are the options?

"Convert salary to hourly" can mean three slightly different calculations, depending on how closely the result needs to match actual hours worked.

  • Standard estimate divides annual salary by 2,080 hours, the figure used for a 40-hour week worked all 52 weeks of the year with no time off subtracted.
  • Adjusted for paid time off subtracts vacation, holidays, and sick days from the standard annual hours first, which raises the effective hourly rate slightly since fewer hours are actually worked for the same salary.
  • Custom work week replaces the 40-hour assumption with the actual weekly schedule, such as 35 or 45 hours, since a shorter or longer standard week changes total annual hours directly.

All three start from the same annual salary figure; the difference comes entirely from how many actual hours that salary gets divided across.

Why the right conversion matters

Using a rounded or generic hours figure doesn't produce an error — it just produces a plausible-looking number that may not reflect what a role actually pays per hour. That shows up in a few common situations:

  • Comparing job offers. A salaried offer and an hourly offer can only be compared fairly once both are expressed the same way, using each role's real annual hours.
  • Freelance and contract rate-setting. Someone leaving a salaried role to freelance needs an hourly baseline that reflects actual hours worked, not a rounded estimate that undercharges for the time.
  • Part-time and reduced-schedule roles. A salary prorated for a 30-hour week produces a very different hourly rate than the same salary divided by a full 40-hour week.
  • Budgeting around unpaid leave. Anyone taking unpaid time off during the year is effectively working fewer hours for the same salary, which raises their real hourly rate for the hours actually worked.
📊 Quick stat The difference between the standard 2,080-hour estimate and a figure adjusted for two weeks of paid time off is about 80 hours a year — enough to shift a calculated hourly rate by roughly 4%, even though the salary itself never changed.

Step-by-step: converting salary to hourly rate

Method 1: Standard estimate (2,080 hours)

  1. Start with the gross annual salary. Use the full pre-tax figure, such as $52,000 a year.
  2. Divide by 2,080 hours. This assumes a 40-hour week worked for all 52 weeks of the year.
    Hourly Rate = Annual Salary ÷ 2,080
  3. Read the result as a rough hourly rate. This figure is a fast estimate, not adjusted for any unpaid time off.

Method 2: Adjusted for paid time off

  1. Count actual paid weeks worked. Subtract vacation weeks, paid holidays, and any other paid leave from 52 total weeks.
  2. Multiply the remaining weeks by weekly hours. For a 40-hour week with 2 weeks of paid time off, that's 50 weeks × 40 hours.
    Adjusted Annual Hours = (52 − PTO Weeks) × Weekly Hours
  3. Divide the salary by that adjusted figure. The result is slightly higher than the standard 2,080-hour estimate, since paid time off doesn't reduce actual pay.
    Hourly Rate = Annual Salary ÷ Adjusted Annual Hours

Method 3: Custom work week

  1. Replace 40 hours with the real weekly schedule. For example, a 37.5-hour or 45-hour standard week.
  2. Multiply weekly hours by 52. This produces the correct total annual hours for that schedule.
    Annual Hours = Weekly Hours × 52
  3. Divide the salary by that total. This gives an hourly rate that matches the actual work week rather than the generic 40-hour assumption.

Common mistakes when converting salary to hourly

1. Using 2,000 hours instead of 2,080

2,000 is a rough round number, not an actual work-year figure; using it instead of 2,080 overstates the hourly rate by about 4%, which compounds into a meaningful gap over a full salary comparison.

2. Ignoring unpaid time off

A role with unpaid leave built in, such as certain teaching or seasonal contracts, has fewer actual paid hours than 2,080, so dividing by the standard figure understates the real hourly rate for the hours actually worked.

3. Assuming every role is a 40-hour week

Plenty of salaried roles run on a 35 or 37.5-hour standard week; applying the 40-hour, 2,080-hour formula to one of these understates the true hourly rate, since fewer hours are actually required for the same pay.

4. Including bonuses or commission in the base figure

Folding a bonus or commission into the annual salary before dividing inflates the hourly rate with pay that isn't guaranteed or hour-based, which skews any direct comparison against a purely hourly role.

💡 Pro tip When comparing a salaried offer to an hourly one, ask specifically how many paid vacation and holiday days come with the salaried role before converting — that number changes the real hourly rate more than most people expect.
