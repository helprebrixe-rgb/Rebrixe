Every Google SERP Feature Explained

By Rebrixe Team · July 24, 2026 · 11 min read

Search "best running shoes" and the page you get back barely resembles ten blue links anymore. There's an image carousel, a row of "People also ask" questions, maybe a shopping pack with prices and star ratings, and your actual organic listing pushed halfway down the screen. Rank #1 doesn't mean what it used to.

The problem is most site owners are optimizing for a results page that stopped existing years ago. Before you can chase a SERP feature, you need to know it exists, what triggers it, and whether it's even something your page can realistically win. That's what this list is for.

Quick Answer

Google SERP features are the non-standard elements that appear on a search results page alongside or instead of regular organic listings — things like featured snippets, People Also Ask boxes, knowledge panels, image packs, and local packs. Each one is triggered by a different combination of query intent, structured data, and content format, and Google decides which feature (if any) appears for a given search.

What is a SERP feature?

A SERP feature is any element on a Google search results page that isn't a plain organic listing — a title, URL, and description in a blue-link format. Instead, SERP features pull content into a richer format: a snippet of text, a strip of images, a map, a set of questions, or an entity panel built from Google's own knowledge graph.

  • They're query-dependent. The same feature won't appear for every search — a knowledge panel shows up for "Marie Curie," not for "how to unclog a drain."
  • Some are earned, some are owned by Google. Featured snippets and image packs pull from ranking webpages, while knowledge panels and unit converters are generated entirely by Google.
  • They can appear together. A single results page might show a PAA box, an image pack, and a local pack all at once, especially for broad or local-intent queries.
  • They shift with intent. Informational queries tend to surface snippets and PAA, transactional queries surface shopping and ad-adjacent packs, and local queries surface maps and local packs.

The practical takeaway: ranking well isn't just about your position in the organic list anymore. It's about knowing which feature is realistically in play for your keyword, and whether your content is even built in a format that feature can use.

Why SERP features matter

Ignoring SERP features when planning content means optimizing for a results page that doesn't match reality:

  • They redistribute clicks. A featured snippet or knowledge panel can answer the query directly on the results page, meaning even a #1 organic ranking sees fewer clicks than it would have five years ago.
  • They can also send clicks your way. Sitelinks, review stars, and FAQ dropdowns attached to your own listing tend to increase click-through rate rather than reduce it.
  • They reveal query intent at a glance. The features present on a SERP are a direct signal of what Google believes the searcher wants — a shopping pack means transactional intent, a PAA box means informational intent.
  • They're winnable with the right format. Featured snippets, image packs, and FAQ rich results are all earned from on-page content and structured data, not paid placement.
📊 Quick stat Most page-one results for informational queries now include at least one non-organic SERP feature above the first blue link — meaning the "top" organic result is frequently the fourth or fifth thing a searcher actually sees on the page.

Step-by-step: auditing your SERP features

  1. Search your target keyword in an incognito window. This avoids personalization skewing what you see versus what most searchers see.
  2. List every feature present on the page. Note anything that isn't a standard organic listing — snippets, packs, panels, carousels, PAA boxes.
  3. Identify which features are winnable. Features sourced from ranking webpages (snippets, PAA, image packs) are earnable; features from Google's own knowledge graph (panels, direct answers) generally aren't.
  4. Check the current snippet or pack holder's format. A paragraph snippet, list snippet, and table snippet each need different on-page structure to compete for.
  5. Match your content structure to the feature. Add a concise answer near the top for paragraph snippets, use ordered lists for list snippets, and mark up FAQs with schema for FAQ-eligible results.
  6. Add relevant structured data. Schema markup doesn't guarantee a feature, but it's required groundwork for most rich results, including review stars and FAQ dropdowns.
  7. Re-check the SERP after publishing. Use a SERP simulator or repeat the manual search to see whether the feature landscape shifted in your favor.

The full list of SERP features

The feature types that most commonly appear across Google search results today, grouped by what they show and where they pull from.

Featured Snippet
A paragraph, list, or table pulled from a ranking page and shown above the standard organic results, credited with a link back to the source.
Example query: "how does hreflang work"
People Also Ask (PAA)
An expandable box of related questions, each revealing a short answer sourced from a webpage when clicked.
Example query: "do I need hreflang"
Image Pack
A horizontal row of images related to the query, appearing within or above organic results, linking through to source pages.
Example query: "mid-century modern living room"
Knowledge Panel
A box on the right (desktop) or top (mobile) showing facts about an entity — person, place, organization — built from Google's knowledge graph.
Example query: "Marie Curie"
Local Pack
A map with three business listings shown for location-based queries, pulling from Google Business Profile data.
Example query: "coffee shops near me"
Shopping Pack
A row of product cards with images, prices, and merchant names, sourced from Google Merchant Center product feeds.
Example query: "wireless headphones under $100"
Sitelinks
Extra links beneath a listing (usually your own site's top-ranking page) pointing to relevant internal pages, shown when Google is confident about your site structure.
Example query: a brand name search, like "rebrixe"
Review Stars (Rich Snippet)
Star ratings shown beneath an organic listing, pulled from review schema markup on the page.
Example query: "best blender 2026"
FAQ Rich Result
An expandable list of questions and answers attached directly to your own organic listing, generated from FAQPage schema.
Example query: "hreflang explained"
Video Carousel
A horizontal row of video thumbnails with duration and source, typically pulled from YouTube or other indexed video content.
Example query: "how to tie a tie"
Top Stories
A carousel of recent news articles for a query with current-events relevance, sourced from Google News-indexed publishers.
Example query: a breaking news topic
AI Overview
An AI-generated summary appearing above organic results, synthesizing information from multiple sources with linked citations.
Example query: "difference between hreflang and canonical"
Direct Answer Box
A short, single-fact answer shown at the top of results for simple factual queries, generated by Google without a source attribution link.
Example query: "what time zone is Tokyo"
Related Searches
A set of alternate query suggestions shown at the bottom of the results page, reflecting how Google clusters related search intent.
Example query: any broad informational search

This isn't an exhaustive list — Google tests and retires niche formats constantly — but it covers the features that show up often enough to be worth planning around.

Common mistakes when chasing SERP features

1. Targeting a feature Google owns, not the page

Knowledge panels and direct answer boxes are built from Google's own data sources, not earned from your webpage — no amount of on-page optimization will win you a placement that was never up for grabs.

2. Ignoring the current snippet's format

If the existing featured snippet is a numbered list, restructuring your content as a single paragraph won't compete for it — matching the format Google is already pulling matters as much as matching the content.

3. Adding schema without matching page content

FAQ schema that references questions and answers not actually visible and matching on the page violates Google's structured data guidelines and can get the rich result suppressed entirely.

4. Assuming every feature increases clicks

Winning a featured snippet or PAA slot can sometimes reduce clicks if the visible answer fully satisfies the searcher — the goal is deciding which features are worth optimizing for your specific business model, not chasing all of them uniformly.

5. Not checking mobile vs. desktop separately

Several features render differently or don't appear at all depending on device, so an audit done only on desktop can miss what most of your actual traffic sees.

💡 Pro tip Before writing new content for a keyword, search it first and note every feature present. Build the page's structure around the ones you can realistically compete for, not around the average blog template.
