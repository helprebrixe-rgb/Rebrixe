Business Days vs Calendar Days: What's the Difference?

By Rebrixe Team · Updated August 11, 2026 · 9 min read

A contract says the refund will land "within 10 days." A shipping label says "arrives in 5 days." A vendor promises to reply "within 3 days" of a support ticket. None of those sentences say whether weekends count, and the answer changes the actual date by anywhere from two days to over a week depending on where the range falls.

This mix-up is one of the most common sources of missed deadlines, disputed invoices, and "where's my package" complaints — not because the math is hard, but because "days" quietly means two different things depending on who's counting, and depending on which country's working week and holiday calendar applies. Here's how to tell business days and calendar days apart, convert between them in seconds, and never miscount a deadline again — whether you're in the US, UK, Canada, Australia, or anywhere else.

Quick Answer

Calendar days count every single day on the calendar, including weekends and holidays. Business days count only the days a typical office operates — usually Monday through Friday in the US, UK, Canada, and Australia — excluding weekends and recognized holidays. Ten calendar days from a Friday lands on the following Sunday-week Monday, while ten business days from that same Friday stretches to two full calendar weeks once both weekends are skipped. As a rule of thumb: business days ≈ calendar days × 5⁄7, and calendar days ≈ business days × 7⁄5 — but for an exact date, always walk the real calendar or use a converter, since public holidays shift the result further.

What are business days and calendar days?

Both terms describe a way of counting a span of time, but they draw the line differently on which days are included.

  • Calendar days include every day of the week — Monday through Sunday — plus every holiday, with no exceptions or skips of any kind. This is the definition most people mean by default when they just say "days."
  • Business days (also called working days) include only the days a standard business operates, which is typically Monday through Friday in the US, UK, Canada, and most of Europe, with weekends and recognized holidays left out of the count entirely.
  • Custom business weeks exist too — the UAE and Saudi Arabia moved to a Monday–Friday week in recent years, but several Middle Eastern countries (Israel, and parts of the Gulf) still run a Sunday–Thursday or Friday–Saturday week, so "business day" ultimately depends on the operating calendar of whoever set the term.

The date range being measured never changes; what changes is which of the days inside that range actually get counted toward the total. That's the entire distinction — and it's also exactly where "10 days" and "10 business days" quietly stop meaning the same thing.

Why the distinction matters

Assuming the wrong type of "day" doesn't produce an obviously wrong number — it produces a number that looks perfectly reasonable and is still off by several days. That shows up in places where the stakes are real:

  • Contracts and legal notices. A "10 days to respond" clause interpreted as business days instead of calendar days can shift a legal deadline by nearly a full week.
  • Shipping and delivery promises. "Ships in 3 days" almost always means business days, so a Friday order won't leave the warehouse until the following Monday or Tuesday, and won't even start its clock if it missed the carrier's daily cutoff time.
  • Payment and refund terms. "Net 30" and refund windows are conventionally calendar days unless the contract explicitly says otherwise, so treating them as business days can make a payment look late or a refund look overdue when it isn't. On a freelance invoice dated the 1st, "Net 30" calendar days is due the 31st — but "Net 30 business days" isn't due until roughly six weeks out.
  • SLAs and support tickets. A "respond within 2 days" SLA measured in calendar days instead of business days can make weekend-heavy tickets look breached when the support team was simply closed.
  • Court filings and regulatory deadlines. Federal and state courts apply specific rules for whether a period counts business or calendar days — get it wrong and a "not an acceptable mistake" missed-deadline problem follows. See the legal section below.
📊 Quick stat A typical calendar month has 28 to 31 days, but only around 20 to 23 of them are business days — roughly two out of every seven days get removed from the count once weekends are excluded, and a public holiday removes one more.

Step-by-step: counting each one correctly

Method 1: Counting calendar days

  1. Identify the start date and the number of days to add or the end date to compare. No filtering is needed — every day counts.
  2. Count forward (or backward) one day at a time, or subtract the two dates directly. In a spreadsheet, this is simply end date − start date.
  3. Include weekends and holidays in the total. Nothing gets skipped, so the result is a straight day span.

Method 2: Counting business days

  1. Identify the start date and the number of business days needed. Decide which days count as the "weekend" for the relevant business — usually Saturday and Sunday in the US, UK, Canada, and Australia.
  2. Walk forward one day at a time, skipping any day that falls on a weekend. Only count a day toward the total if it's a weekday.
  3. Skip recognized holidays as well. If the business or region observes public holidays, remove those dates from the count too, even though they fall on a weekday.
  4. In a spreadsheet, use a built-in function instead of counting by hand.
    =WORKDAY(A2,10) ' returns the date 10 business days after A2 =NETWORKDAYS(A2,B2) ' returns the number of business days between A2 and B2 =NETWORKDAYS.INTL(A2,B2,11) ' same, but lets you set a custom weekend (e.g. Fri–Sat)

What "next business day" actually means

"Next business day" is the very next weekday that isn't a recognized holiday — if today is Friday, the next business day is Monday, and if Monday is a public holiday, it rolls to Tuesday. That part is simple. The part that trips people up is the cutoff time most services attach to the promise.

  • Before the cutoff: the current day counts as "day zero," and the countdown starts immediately.
  • After the cutoff: the request is treated as if it arrived the following business day, which pushes every downstream date out by one extra day.
  • Weekend or holiday submissions: almost always roll forward to the next open business day before the clock starts at all.

This is why an order placed Friday evening with "next business day shipping" doesn't ship Saturday, or even Monday morning — it often doesn't leave the warehouse until Tuesday, because Friday missed the cutoff and Monday becomes day zero. The same logic applies to bank transfers, e-signature turnarounds, and customer support SLAs: always check the stated cutoff time and time zone before assuming "next business day" means less than 48 hours from right now.

Legal deadlines: how courts count days (US & UK)

Legal contexts don't use one rule consistently, which makes them the highest-stakes place to get this wrong.

United States — Federal Rules of Civil Procedure

Under FRCP Rule 6, a "day" generally means a calendar day unless the rule or an order says otherwise. Historically, periods under 11 days excluded weekends and federal holidays, while periods of 11 days or more counted every calendar day — a 2009 amendment simplified this so that, in most modern federal contexts, all periods now count calendar days by default, and it's the rule or statute itself that must expressly say "business days" for weekends to be excluded. If a deadline lands on a weekend, legal holiday, or a day the clerk's office is closed, it automatically rolls forward to the next business day. State courts often follow similar logic but are not required to, so always confirm the specific rule for your jurisdiction rather than assuming the federal approach applies.

United Kingdom — "working days" in statute and contract law

UK statutes and contracts more commonly use the term "working days" rather than "business days," and it typically excludes weekends and bank holidays in the relevant part of the UK (England & Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland each observe slightly different bank holidays). Consumer rights notices, redundancy consultation periods, and many government response-time guarantees are stated in working days for this reason — always check which nation's bank holiday calendar the contract or notice is anchored to when parties are spread across the UK.

⚖️ Not legal advice Rules vary by jurisdiction, court, and specific statute, and they change over time. For any filing, notice period, or regulatory deadline that carries real consequences, confirm the applicable rule with the relevant court, agency, or a qualified professional rather than relying on a general guide.

Common mistakes people make

1. Assuming "days" always means business days

Most legal and financial terms default to calendar days unless "business days" or "working days" is stated explicitly, so assuming the friendlier interpretation can push a real deadline earlier than expected.

2. Forgetting holidays inside the range

A business day count that only skips weekends but ignores a holiday sitting in the middle of the range will come out one or more days short of the true number of operating days.

3. Miscounting the start date

Whether "day one" is the start date itself or the day after it is a common ambiguity — contracts and carriers don't all define it the same way, so the same phrase can produce two different valid due dates.

4. Using a flat multiplier instead of walking the actual dates

There's no fixed ratio for converting calendar days to business days, because the result depends entirely on where the weekends and holidays happen to fall within that specific range — a rough "divide by 1.4" estimate can be off by several days.

5. Ignoring the cutoff time on "next business day" promises

Placing an order or submitting a form minutes after a carrier's or provider's daily cutoff silently shifts day zero forward by an entire day, which is easy to miss if you're only counting from the calendar date and not the exact time.

💡 Pro tip When a deadline genuinely matters, don't estimate — walk the actual calendar or use a function like NETWORKDAYS or WORKDAY that accounts for the real weekends and holidays in that specific date range.

International variations: weekends aren't universal

"Business days" assumes a Monday–Friday week, but that's a regional convention, not a global constant — worth checking before applying US or UK assumptions to an international deadline.

Region Business week Weekend
US, UK, Canada, Australia, most of Europe Monday–Friday Saturday–Sunday
UAE, Saudi Arabia (since 2022) Monday–Friday Saturday–Sunday
Israel Sunday–Thursday Friday–Saturday
Egypt, Qatar, Kuwait Sunday–Thursday Friday–Saturday
Some six-day operating industries Monday–Saturday Sunday only

For cross-border contracts, always confirm both parties are counting against the same business week and the same public holiday calendar — a Friday deadline treated as a working day in Dubai but a weekend day in London is a common, avoidable dispute.
