How to Calculate In Hand Salary from Your CTC (2026 Guide)

By Rebrixe Team · July 28, 2026 · 10 min read

You get an offer letter with a shiny CTC number on it, do a quick mental math, and picture a certain amount landing in your account every month. Then the first payslip arrives, and it's noticeably less than what you expected — sometimes by a few thousand rupees, sometimes by a lot more.

That gap isn't a mistake. It's EPF, professional tax, TDS, and a handful of other deductions your employer is required to subtract before your salary ever reaches your bank account. The confusing part is that none of this is usually explained clearly in the offer letter itself — you're just handed a CTC figure and left to guess. This guide breaks down exactly what happens between "CTC" and "in hand salary," in plain numbers, so you can calculate your real take-home pay before you sign anything.

Quick Answer

In hand salary is your CTC minus employer-side costs that never reach you (like employer EPF and gratuity), minus your own deductions (employee EPF, professional tax, and income tax/TDS). For most salaried employees in 2026, take-home pay works out to roughly 70–85% of CTC. The fastest way to get an accurate number is: Gross Salary = CTC − Employer EPF − Gratuity, then In Hand = Gross Salary − Employee EPF − Professional Tax − TDS.

What is in hand salary?

In hand salary — also called take-home salary or net salary — is the exact amount that gets credited to your bank account each month, after every deduction has already been subtracted. It's the number that actually matters for budgeting, rent, EMIs, and everyday spending, and it's almost always smaller than the CTC figure quoted in your offer letter.

  • CTC (Cost to Company). The total yearly cost your employer bears for employing you — salary components plus employer contributions to EPF, gratuity, and sometimes insurance. It's a cost figure, not a payment figure.
  • Gross salary. Your CTC minus the employer-side contributions that never touch your account (employer EPF, gratuity). This is the amount your payslip is actually calculated from.
  • Net / in hand salary. Gross salary minus your own deductions — employee EPF, professional tax, and TDS (tax deducted at source). This is what hits your bank account.
  • Why the three numbers differ. Each one answers a different question: what the company spends, what your salary is calculated on, and what you actually receive. Job offers usually lead with CTC because it's the largest, most impressive-looking number.

Why understanding this matters

Knowing how to translate CTC into in hand salary isn't just a payroll curiosity — it changes real decisions:

  • Comparing job offers fairly. Two offers with the same CTC can have very different in-hand pay depending on how much sits in basic pay, allowances, or employer EPF — the CTC number alone can be misleading.
  • Budgeting and EMI planning. Loan EMIs, rent, and monthly commitments should be planned against your in-hand salary, not your CTC, or you risk overcommitting.
  • Negotiating smarter. Understanding the breakup lets you ask for a specific in-hand number during negotiation instead of just accepting a CTC figure at face value.
  • Avoiding payslip surprises. When you already know roughly what to expect, an unexpected drop in your payslip is something you can question immediately instead of discovering months later.
📊 Quick stat Under the new tax regime for FY 2026-27, income tax is effectively nil for taxable income up to ₹12 lakh (after a ₹75,000 standard deduction, that's roughly ₹12.75 lakh gross salary) — but EPF and professional tax still apply below that threshold, so "no tax" does not mean "no deductions."

Step-by-step: calculating your in hand salary

  1. Start with your CTC. Find the exact annual CTC figure from your offer letter or appointment letter — not a rounded number, the precise one, since small differences compound across every step below.
  2. Subtract employer-side contributions to get gross salary. Remove the employer's EPF contribution (usually 12% of basic pay) and gratuity provision (roughly 4.81% of basic pay) from the CTC — these are costs to the company, not money paid to you.
  3. Identify your basic pay. Most structures set basic pay at 40–50% of CTC or gross salary. This single number drives your EPF deduction and often your HRA and gratuity calculations too.
  4. Subtract your own EPF contribution. As an employee, you also contribute 12% of basic pay (up to the statutory wage ceiling) to EPF — this comes out of your gross salary directly.
  5. Subtract professional tax, if applicable. Some states charge a small monthly professional tax, commonly around ₹200/month, capped by law at ₹2,500 per year. Not every state levies this.
  6. Calculate income tax (TDS) for the year. Apply the standard deduction (₹75,000 under the new regime for FY 2026-27), then run the remaining taxable income through the slab rates, factoring in the rebate that zeroes out tax up to ₹12 lakh taxable income. Divide the annual tax by 12 for the monthly TDS.
  7. Add it all up. In Hand Salary = Gross Salary − Employee EPF − Professional Tax − Monthly TDS. What's left is the number that actually reaches your bank account.

Common mistakes people make

1. Comparing offers using CTC alone

A higher CTC doesn't automatically mean higher take-home pay. If one offer packs more into employer EPF, gratuity, or non-cash perks, its in-hand salary can end up lower than a smaller CTC with a leaner structure.

2. Forgetting that EPF cuts both ways

People often account for the employer's EPF contribution being excluded from take-home pay, but forget that their own 12% employee EPF contribution is also deducted before the salary reaches their account — it's easy to double-count the "same" 12% as only affecting one side.

3. Assuming "no tax" means "no deductions"

The tax rebate that zeroes out income tax up to ₹12 lakh (new regime, FY 2026-27) only affects TDS. EPF and professional tax are calculated independently and still apply, so take-home pay is still meaningfully lower than gross salary even at income levels with zero tax.

4. Ignoring which tax regime applies by default

The new tax regime is the default unless you actively opt for the old regime, and the two produce different in-hand numbers depending on how many deductions and exemptions you can claim. Calculating under the wrong regime gives a misleading result.

💡 Pro tip Ask HR for the full salary breakup — basic, HRA, special allowance, employer EPF, and gratuity — before accepting an offer. A CTC number without a breakup makes it impossible to estimate your real in-hand salary.
